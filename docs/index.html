Two ways banks fail

Banks die slowly from bad loans, or quickly from a run on uninsured deposits. A screen built for one kind is nearly blind to the other. I tested that idea on every US bank from 1985 to 2026, with the rules written down and committed before any outcome was computed.

Credit lens problem loans against capital, the classic Texas ratio. Run lens capital left after securities losses, plus uninsured deposits per dollar of liquid assets. Failure model 29 ratios across seven risk dimensions, with the direction of each ratio fixed by theory.

2023: the run lens saw the panic coming. The credit lens did not.

Each bank was scored on its December 2022 report, public in mid-February, a month before Silicon Valley Bank failed. The outcome is what happened to its uninsured deposits in the first quarter of 2023. Banks that failed before they could file count as losing everything.

Where the 2023 failures sat

Every bank at the end of 2022, placed by the two halves of the run lens. Further left means less capital left after marking securities to market. Higher means more uninsured deposits for every dollar the bank could quickly raise. The shaded corner is fragile on both.

The run lens marks a standing weakness, not a countdown. SVB ranked between the 85th and 88th percentile on it every quarter from 2019, because most of its deposits were always uninsured. What changed in 2022 was the capital half: rate rises erased it at market prices. A screen like this tells you who to watch, not when.

2008: the credit lens wins

The same two screens, scored on December 2007 reports, against which banks failed by the end of 2011. That crisis was mostly bad real-estate loans, and it shows.

    The failure model: theory as a constraint

    The two lenses are three ratios between them. The failure model covers what a supervisor actually rates: capital (including capital after loans and securities are marked to market), asset quality, earnings, liquidity and funding, rate sensitivity, depositors, and growth and concentration. That is 29 ratios.

    It is a gradient-boosted model with one rule built in. Wherever theory says which way a ratio cuts, the model is forbidden from learning the opposite. Thinner capital or more uninsured deposits can never lower a bank's risk score. That was the exact mistake of the first version.

    To test it fairly, I trained it on one crisis and tested it on another it never saw. Every chart below uses the same scale.

    Run risk needed its own fix

    A model trained on ordinary quarters when a bank's uninsured deposits fell 20% learned the wrong thing. That kind of drop is mostly noise at tiny banks, so the model learned "small bank" and ranked the 2023 Fed borrowers worse than chance. Comparing each bank only with banks its own size fixed it. On the 2008 deposit runs, which no version had touched, the fixed model became the strongest signal. On 2023, which had already been seen, the simple run lens still won.

    The 2026 test, and where improvement stopped

    After that, I tried twelve more ideas under a fixed rule: keep one only if it raises cross-validated accuracy inside the training years by a set margin, in most eras. Nothing passed two rounds running, so the search stopped there. The best idea added 0.0003 against a required 0.002. Every attempt is logged. Scores for every bank on its June 2026 report are committed with a fingerprint, so they can be checked against the failures still to come.

    What the first version got wrong

    The first attempt trained a single model on 2001–2018 and tested it, frozen, on 2019–2024. It caught 10 of 18 failures, about as many as the Texas ratio alone. It missed SVB, Signature and First Republic completely. From the 2008 era it had learned that big securities portfolios and uninsured deposits were safe, because in 2008 they were.

    How it was kept honest

    Rules before results

    Every lens, outcome, metric and hypothesis was written into a preregistration file and committed to git before any outcome was computed. The commit history shows the order.

    Thousands of banks, not three

    The tests use every bank: 4,662 in 2023, 7,788 in 2008, 15,382 in 1990. SVB, Signature and First Republic are illustrations, not the evidence.

    Fresh data for every claim

    Each improvement was judged on data no earlier version had used: the early-1990s crisis, the 2008 deposit runs, Federal Reserve emergency-loan records and the 2026 failures.

    What is still hindsight

    I picked the run-lens mechanism knowing 2023 happened. The defences are zero fitted parameters, whole-population tests, a 2008 test it was expected to lose, and a calm-quarter placebo it had to pass.

    Effects are modest

    These screens shift the odds and tell a supervisor where to look. A flag means look closer, not that failure is coming.

    Bugs and flaws, left in view

    Two unit errors were found after results were in; both runs are shown and every conclusion held. The blind analyst caught a flaw in the credit lens: in 2022, securities losses inside book equity made it partly a rate-loss measure.

    Analyst memos, checked

    A Claude agent wrote each memo from anonymized numbers only. A script checked all 492 numbers the memos cite against the source data.

    What was not tested

    Regulators' enforcement actions have no bulk download. The two-signal rule cannot run on 1990, before securities values were reported. Deposit-franchise value was left out.
