Two ways banks fail

Banks die slowly from bad loans, or quickly from a run on uninsured deposits. A screen built for one kind is nearly blind to the other. I tested two simple screens on every US bank, through the 2008 crisis and the 2023 panic, with the rules written down before any outcome was computed.

Credit lens: problem loans against capital (the Texas ratio). Run lens: capital after unrealized securities losses, plus uninsured deposits per dollar of liquid assets. Neither lens has a single fitted parameter.

2023: the run lens saw the panic coming. The credit lens did not.

Each bank was scored on its December 2022 report, public in mid-February, a month before Silicon Valley Bank failed. The outcome is what happened to its uninsured deposits in the first quarter of 2023. Banks that failed before they could file count as losing everything.

2008: the credit lens wins

The same two screens, scored on December 2007 reports, against which banks failed by the end of 2011. That crisis was mostly bad real-estate loans, and it shows.

Where the 2023 failures sat

Every bank at the end of 2022, placed by the two parts of the run lens. Further right means more capital left after marking securities to market. Higher means more uninsured deposits for every dollar the bank could quickly raise.

The run lens marks a standing weakness, not a countdown. SVB ranked between the 85th and 88th percentile on the run lens every quarter from 2019, because most of its deposits were always uninsured. What changed in 2022 was the capital half: rate rises erased it at market prices. A screen like this tells you who to watch, not when.

Look up a bank

Both lenses for every US bank, quarter by quarter, 2019–2024, beside the risk rank from the machine-learned model of the first version. Percentiles are against all banks that quarter; 100 is the most fragile. History stops at the end of 2024 on purpose: this is a backtest, not a live rating.

What the first version got wrong

The first attempt trained a single model on 2001–2018 bank data and tested it, frozen, on 2019–2024. It caught 10 of 18 failures, about as many as the Texas ratio alone. It missed SVB, Signature and First Republic completely. From the 2008 era it had learned that big securities portfolios and uninsured deposits were safe, because in 2008 they were. The failure is the reason for the two-lens design: one score cannot learn both kinds of failure from history that contains mostly one kind.

How it was kept honest

Rules before results

Every lens, outcome, metric and hypothesis was written into a preregistration file and committed to git before any outcome was computed. The commit history shows the order.

Nothing fitted

Both lenses are percentile ranks of ratios defined by supervisory practice and bank-run theory. There are no weights to tune toward the answer.

Thousands of banks, not three

The tests use every bank: 4,662 in 2023, 7,788 in 2008, 5,345 in the placebo. SVB, Signature and First Republic are illustrations, not the evidence.

What is still hindsight

I picked the run-lens mechanism knowing 2023 happened. The defences are the zero parameters, the cross-sections, the 2008 test it was expected to lose, and the calm-quarter placebo it had to pass.

Effects are modest

The rank correlations are small (0.07 in 2023). These are screens that shift the odds and tell a supervisor where to look. They do not predict which bank fails next.

Analyst memos, checked

A Claude agent wrote each memo blind, from anonymized numbers only. A script then checked all 452 numbers the memos cite; every one appears in the source data.

A flaw the memos caught

The blind analyst noticed that five of the credit lens's top banks for 2022 had negative book equity but healthy regulatory capital. Securities losses run through book equity, which the Texas ratio uses. So in 2022 the credit lens partly measured rate losses, not bad loans. The results above are left as preregistered.

Data: FDIC BankFind quarterly financials and failure list, as amended. Report figures count as public 45 days after quarter end. Loans are not marked to market, because call reports do not include loan fair values. This is a research backtest, not a rating of any bank, and not investment advice.
